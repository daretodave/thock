Every keyboard on a desk collects the same debris, whether it's a budget office board or a four-figure custom build: skin oil, dust, crumbs from lunch eaten too close to the keys. None of it ruins a keystroke on its own. It accumulates quietly, and the board that gets a light pass on a fixed schedule feels different, years in, from the one that never does.

This is the maintenance guide, not the build guide — it assumes switches are already lubed the way the builder wants and focuses on keeping that state. For the mod itself, see [lubing switches](/article/lubing-101) and the [stabilizer servicing guide](/article/stabilizer-servicing-guide).

## Why maintenance matters

Dust settles into switch gaps and stabilizer wells, where it mixes with lube over months and turns from a fine powder into a gritty paste — the kind that produces a faint scratch on keystrokes that used to be smooth.

Skin oil transfers from fingertips to keycap tops on every stroke, and on the heaviest-use keys — home row, space bar, the WASD cluster on a gaming board — it slowly polishes the plastic into keycap shine: a glossy patch where the molded texture used to be. <Mono>ABS</Mono> shines faster than <Mono>PBT</Mono> because the polymer is softer, but no material is fully immune over a long timeline.

Ambient moisture is a quieter risk. A humid room or a drink set down too close can encourage oxidation on exposed contacts — stabilizer wires, switch leaves, PCB traces — which is a common cause of a switch that used to register cleanly and now double-types or drops keys. A dry environment and periodic dusting head most of this off before it starts.

## A regular light-cleaning routine

Most keyboard maintenance isn't deep intervention. It's a short, boring routine repeated often enough that debris never builds into something that needs disassembly.

**A manual pump duster, or compressed air used carefully.** Either clears loose dust and crumbs from between keycaps and out of the switch gaps. A squeeze-bulb duster is the safer default — canned air can spit cold propellant residue if the can tips off vertical. Short bursts, board tilted so debris falls away rather than deeper in.

**A soft brush for switch gaps and stabilizer wells.** An anti-static brush or a clean paintbrush reaches into the gap around each stem and into stabilized-key wells, where crumbs settle in ways a duster alone can't dislodge.

**Keycap puller technique.** A wire-style puller straddling the keycap from two sides and lifting straight up is safer than a ring-style puller, which can twist and mark softer legends. Pulling a handful of heaviest-use keys periodically catches debris before it changes the feel.

**A microfiber wipe for the case.** A dry or barely-damp microfiber cloth across the top plate and deck removes the handling-oil film without the scratch risk of a rougher cloth.

**Cadence.** A quick weekly pass — duster and a wipe-down, under a minute — keeps loose debris from settling. A monthly pass with the brush reaching into stabilizer wells and a spot-check keycap pull catches what the weekly pass misses. Heavy daily use or a dusty, food-adjacent desk warrants tightening that schedule.

## Deep cleaning

Deep cleaning is a keycap-off session, worth doing when shine is visible, when six months or more have passed since the last one, or when debris shows up under the keycaps even after a light pass.

**Keycap soak.** Pull the set and soak in mild soap and water — a few drops of dish soap in a basin handles most of the oil film and grime. For pad-printed or laser-etched legends rather than dye-sublimated or doubleshot ones, a soft wipe beats a scrub; a cotton swab dampened with isopropyl alcohol works well for spot-cleaning those specifically.

**Dry completely before reinstalling.** Trapped water under a keycap or in the stem cavity doesn't evaporate quickly, and reinstalling while damp invites a musty smell days later. A full day of air-drying stem-side up on a towel is the safer default.

**Hot-swap sockets versus desoldering.** Hot-swap lets a single contaminated or scratchy switch come out in seconds with no soldering — the main maintenance advantage of the format. A soldered board needs desoldering to pull a switch, which most builders reserve for switches that are actually failing rather than routine cleaning. Desoldering the whole board just to wipe housings from outside is rarely worth it.

<Callout type="warn" title="Skip the ultrasonic cleaner">
  Ultrasonic cleaners work well on keycaps alone but are a poor fit for switches. The cavitation that lifts grime off a keycap can also strip lube off internal switch surfaces, and ultrasonic baths risk pushing liquid past the seams of an assembled switch and onto the leaf — an electrical contact that soap-and-water cleaning isn't meant to touch. Keeping switches out of the bath entirely is the fix, not avoiding ultrasonic cleaning altogether.
</Callout>

## Liquid spill response

A spill is the one scenario where speed matters more than technique. What happens in the first thirty seconds decides more than anything done afterward.

<InlineViz
  src="/article-viz/keyboard-cleaning-maintenance-guide/spill-response-steps.svg"

Panel 4: a dropper bottle releasing drops onto a switch. Panel 5: the keyboard sitting upright surrounded by short radiating dash marks suggesting air-drying."
  caption="The spill sequence: power off first, dry-out time last — nothing in between shortcuts the wait."
  accent="amber"
/>

**Power down and disconnect immediately.** Unplug the cable, or hold a wireless board's power switch off. Liquid bridging two live contacts is what actually damages components — a board disconnected fast enough often survives a spill that would have shorted something out live.

**Flip the board and pull keycaps.** Tipping it lets standing liquid drain away from the switches instead of pooling, and removing keycaps — at least in the affected area — exposes the stems and stabilizer wells to air and to direct cleaning.

**Blot, don't wipe.** A cloth pressed straight down onto standing liquid pulls it up; a sweeping wipe pushes it sideways into housings and wells it hadn't reached.

**Isopropyl flush for sticky residue.** Water blots up cleanly, but soda, juice, or sweetened coffee leaves a sticky film once the water evaporates — the real cause of keys that stick well after the spill itself is gone. A swab or syringe with <Mono>90%+ isopropyl alcohol</Mono>, worked into the switch stems and stabilizer wells, cuts through it.

**Full dry-out before reassembly.** Measured in days, not hours — a switch or PCB can feel dry on the outside while holding moisture inside a housing. Reassembling and powering on too early is how a recoverable spill turns into a switch or PCB that has to be replaced. A fan on the open board, or a sealed container with desiccant, speeds the process without adding heat.

<Callout type="note" title="Skip the hairdryer">
  Direct heat can warp keycaps and case plastic, and blowing hot air across residual liquid risks baking it onto the contact leaf rather than removing it — turning a cleanable mess into a switch that needs replacing.
</Callout>

Expectations matter here. A fast response to a plain-water spill often saves a board completely. A sugary or acidic spill, even with a fast response, has a real chance of leaving switches that need replacing rather than salvaging — a cheap fix on a hot-swap board, a repair job on a soldered one.

## Longevity habits

The habits that matter most are the boring ones.

**Keep food and drink away from the desk.** The single highest-leverage habit here, because it prevents the spill scenario above from coming up at all.

**Match cleaning method to case material.** Aluminum tolerates a damp microfiber wipe-down — watch for standing moisture around exposed fasteners, which can corrode if left wet repeatedly. Plastic and polycarbonate cases scratch more easily under an abrasive cloth, so a soft microfiber matters more there. Anodized finishes can dull under harsh solvents; mild soap and water is the safer choice for a case exterior, keeping isopropyl alcohol for legends and switch contacts.

**Know when re-lubing is worth it again.** A board that sounded quiet and turns scratchy after a long stretch of use, or after a deep clean that involved pulling switches, is a reasonable candidate for a light re-lube rather than a full rebuild — see [lubing switches](/article/lubing-101) for the steps. This is occasional, triggered by a change in feel, not a fixed schedule.

**Store idle boards deliberately.** A dust cover or case bag, stable moderate humidity rather than a damp closet, and no direct sunlight — UV exposure yellows <Mono>ABS</Mono> keycaps over time, covered in more detail in [retrobrighting keycaps](/article/retrobrighting-keycaps).

No single habit here does much alone. Stacked together — food kept off the desk, a weekly dusting pass, a monthly deeper check, a fast reaction to spills — they add up to a board that keeps its feel for years instead of months. Pair every keycap-off session with a quick stabilizer check: debris in the wells is a common cause of a stab that used to be quiet developing a new rattle, and the [stabilizer servicing guide](/article/stabilizer-servicing-guide) covers the fix.
