Eight straight weeks of climb — 40, 45, 52, 58, 63, 68, 74, 80 — made <Mono>Wooting</Mono> the top mover on the [2026-W39 trends tracker](/trends/tracker/2026-W39), the sharpest positive swing of any row on the board that week. The catalyst is concrete: <Mono>80HE+</Mono> preorders opened 2026-09-17 with a split spacebar, a new tuning-knob accessory, and a <Mono>Wootility 5.0</Mono> software release alongside it. None of that is a new sensing technology. It's a hardware refresh from a company whose actual differentiator, at this point in the Hall-effect category's life, is the software.

## The mover: a hardware refresh, not a hardware first

Wooting was the vendor that made per-key adjustable actuation and rapid trigger a mainstream keyboard conversation in the first place, and the preorder, listed on <Source href="https://wooting.io">Wooting's own site</Source>, reads like a company iterating on a category it already owns rather than one defending new ground. A split spacebar and a knob are accessory-tier additions — useful, but not the kind of feature that resets a competitive field. What moved the tracker score 40 points in two months is closer to reputation compounding: a vendor with a multi-year track record on rapid-trigger tuning shipping a refresh, with a buying audience that already trusts the firmware behind it.

A category score that high, held by multiple vendors' hardware at once, is a category where the sensing technology itself has stopped being the pitch.

## Hardware parity arrived first

The switch hardware argument that used to separate Wooting from everyone else has largely closed. The <PartReference id="gateron-magnetic-jade">Gateron Magnetic Jade</PartReference>, on shelves since September 2022, put a rare-earth magnet in the stem in place of Wooting's own <Mono>Lekker</Mono> switch design and made digitally adjustable actuation something any vendor with hot-swap sockets could offer. Keychron went further: the <PartReference id="keychron-q1-he">Q1 HE 8K Marble</PartReference> reached reviewers in April 2026 running Hall-effect magnetic switches over <Mono>ZMK</Mono> firmware at an 8000Hz polling claim, and by September 2026 Keychron had opened a Kickstarter for the <Mono>Q16 HE 8K</Mono>, built around a ceramic-housed TMR (tunnel magnetoresistance) sensor — a physically different read of the same magnet-and-stem mechanism, and one Wooting's own lineup hasn't adopted. Cherry's gaming sub-brand XTRFY shipped its own TMR switch, the <Mono>MK Crystal TMR</Mono>, in the same window. On paper, a reader comparing spec sheets across these boards in September 2026 finds adjustable actuation, hot-swap sockets, and in some cases sensor architecture that's arguably more advanced than what Wooting ships.

That gap in field time is the whole argument. The <PartReference id="wooting-60he">Wooting 60HE</PartReference> entered mass production in April 2022 running the same <Mono>Wootility</Mono> stack — since revised, not replaced — that the 80HE+ ships with today, which puts roughly four and a half years of real-world rapid-trigger tuning, bug reports, and per-key curve iteration behind the software a buyer gets in the box. Keychron's Q1 HE 8K had been in reviewers' hands for roughly five months as of the 2026-W39 tracker read. The Q16 HE 8K hadn't shipped at all — Keychron opened its Kickstarter campaign in September 2026, with the board still at the preorder stage. Hardware parity is a spec sheet. Firmware maturity is time-in-market, and there's no group-buy pledge or Kickstarter stretch goal that compresses it.

What hasn't converged is the configurator itself. Wootility's per-key actuation curves, its rapid-trigger sensitivity tuning, and its analog-axis output to games are the product of years of a company iterating on one specific problem — reading a magnet's field position precisely enough, and fast enough, to make a digital keyboard behave like an analog input device without introducing false triggers. Newer entrants running ZMK or a vendor's first-generation configurator are solving that same problem from further back on the curve, regardless of how capable the underlying sensor is. A TMR sensor reading stem position more precisely than a Hall-effect sensor doesn't automatically translate into a rapid-trigger curve that feels as consistent under repeated taps — that's a firmware and calibration problem sitting on top of the sensor, not a property of the sensor itself.

## The honest counter-argument

That doesn't make Wooting's position permanent. Keychron committing to TMR — a sensing generation Wooting hasn't shipped — is a real technical move, not a catch-up gesture; it's plausible a more precise sensor eventually gives a competitor's firmware team a better foundation to tune against than Wooting's own Hall-effect hardware offers. And software gaps close faster than hardware ones once a vendor decides to invest: Keychron's willingness to ship three distinct magnetic-switch architectures inside a single year says a company treating this as a priority, not an afterthought. Wooting's lead, measured on the 2026-W39 tracker, is real. It is not, by its nature, permanent — it's a lead built on time in market, and every month a competitor's firmware stays in the field is a month that gap narrows.

## What to watch

The tracker row to watch next isn't Wooting's — it's whether <Mono>TMR</Mono>, sitting at 63 and climbing on the 2026-W39 read, starts pulling review language away from actuation-point adjustability and toward calibration consistency and per-key curve behavior. That's the point at which the conversation moves from "does the board have rapid trigger" to "whose rapid trigger is better tuned," and it's the question Wooting's multi-year firmware head start was built to answer before anyone else was asking it seriously. Whether <Mono>Wootility 5.0</Mono> extends that lead or a competitor's configurator finally closes it is the more interesting story sitting underneath the 2026-09-17 preorder headline.
