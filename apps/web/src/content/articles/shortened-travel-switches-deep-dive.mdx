Two switches can share nearly the same spring curve and still feel like different categories of thing under the finger. The usual suspect gets blamed first — "it must be a heavier spring" — but spring weight is often not what changed at all. What changed is how far the stem had left to go. That's a geometry problem, not a force problem, and it's worth taking apart carefully because the two get talked about as if they were the same lever.

## The 4.0mm default, and what gets cut

The MX-style switch that most of the hobby's catalog is still built around travels 4.0mm from rest to bottom-out, with the actuation point sitting 2.0mm into that stroke — Cherry's own <Source href="https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/a/9/b/6/2/MX2A_RED-Datasheet-EN_US.pdf">MX2A Red datasheet</Source> specs exactly that split: 2.0mm pre-travel, 4.0mm total. The <PartReference id="cherry-mx2a-red">Cherry MX2A Red</PartReference> in thock's catalog carries the identical number, 4.0mm total travel, at a 45g actuation climbing to a 75g bottom-out.

A shortened-travel switch keeps the same basic cross and rail architecture but cuts that total stroke down. The <PartReference id="akko-v3-cream-blue-pro">Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro</PartReference> is a clean example: a tactile switch with a PC top housing, nylon bottom housing, and POM stem, factory-lubed, specced at 45g actuation and 55g bottom-out — and 3.3mm of total travel, a bit more than three-quarters of the MX norm. It's not alone in doing this. The <PartReference id="gazzew-boba-u4t">Gazzew Boba U4T</PartReference>, a switch built on an entirely different bump mechanism, ships a 3.2mm stroke off a longer-pole stem design, at 62g actuation and 68g bottom-out. Two switches, two different vendors, two different ways of getting there, both landing in the same shortened-travel territory below 3.5mm.

What's notable about that comparison is what it does not show a pattern in: spring weight. The <Mono>Cherry MX2A Red</Mono>'s 45g/75g curve, the <Mono>Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro</Mono>'s 45g/55g curve, and the <Mono>Gazzew Boba U4T</Mono>'s 62g/68g curve don't move together with travel distance. Shortened travel isn't a spring category. It's a separate spec entirely, one that happens to get cut independently of whatever the spring is doing.

## The runway: why distance matters as much as force

Here's the mechanical piece that force numbers alone don't capture. Once a switch actuates, the stem doesn't stop — it keeps moving until it hits the bottom of the housing. The distance it covers in that last stretch is effectively a runway: room for the stem to keep accelerating, or for the spring to keep loading, before something solid stops it. A longer runway means more distance over which the stem's momentum and the spring's rising resistance can play out before bottom-out arrives. A shorter runway means that same motion gets compressed into less travel, with less room to spread out.

On a standard 4.0mm MX-style switch, that post-actuation runway is roughly 2.0mm — the gap between the 2.0mm actuation point and the 4.0mm floor. Akko doesn't publish a separate pre-travel figure for the V3 Cream Blue Pro, so the exact split isn't available, but the ceiling is fixed by the total: whatever the pre-travel is, the remaining runway cannot exceed 1.3mm, and in practice sits close to it, since shortened-travel tactiles generally keep pre-travel in the same neighborhood as the MX norm and take the cut almost entirely out of the back half of the stroke. Call it a runway cut by a third or more, even without an exact figure to the tenth of a millimeter.

That shorter runway is what makes a shortened-travel switch read as snappier even when its force numbers are unremarkable. The <PartReference id="akko-v3-cream-blue-pro">Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro</PartReference>'s 45g actuation and 55g bottom-out are not a heavy spring by any catalog measure — plenty of standard-travel linears and tactiles sit in the same range or heavier. What's different is how little room the stem has left to decelerate once the tactile bump resolves. The finger reaches the floor sooner, on less motion, which reads as quickness even though the resistance it's pushing against is ordinary. Perceived speed, in other words, is partly a function of distance, not just of force — and that's the piece a spec sheet listing only gram weights doesn't show.

## Two different levers, not one

This is the distinction worth being precise about, because it's the one that gets collapsed constantly in build threads: swapping to a heavier spring and shortening the travel are not the same move, and they don't produce the same result.

A heavier spring raises the force required at every point along the stroke — initial force, bottom-out force, or both, depending on how the spring is wound. It does not change how far the stem travels to get there. Put a 67g spring in a 4.0mm housing and the stem still has to cover all 4.0mm; it just takes more force to cover it. The geometry of the press is untouched. [A dedicated piece on spring swaps](/article/spring-swaps-explained) covers this in detail — the spring controls initial force, bottom-out force, and curve shape, full stop, and nothing about the distance the stem has to cross.

Shortening travel does the opposite: it changes the distance without necessarily touching the force curve at all. The <Mono>Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro</Mono>'s 45g/55g spring isn't unusually light or heavy — it's a fairly ordinary tactile curve sitting inside a stroke that's roughly 18 percent shorter than the MX default. The snappiness builders report from switches like this is a runway effect, not a force effect, and it would show up even if the exact same spring were installed in a full 4.0mm housing — it just wouldn't feel the same way, because there'd be more distance for the stem to spread that resistance over.

Put plainly: a heavier spring makes a switch harder to press over the same distance. A shorter stroke makes the same press happen over less distance, no matter what the spring is doing. Builders chasing "snappier" by spring-swapping alone are pulling the wrong lever if what they actually want is less runway.

## Where this shows up

Shortened-travel tactiles like the <PartReference id="akko-v3-cream-blue-pro">Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro</PartReference> have become a common pick for budget tactile builds specifically because the format delivers a quicker-feeling keystroke without requiring an unusually stiff spring or a specialized stem. Akko's own description of the switch — a light tactile with a sharp early bump and a 3.3mm shortened travel — lines up with that use case directly: a sharp tactile event, resolved early, with little runway left afterward to soften the landing. It's listed through NovelKeys's catalog, factory-lubed out of the box, and was released April 2023 — a cheap, ready-to-type option rather than a switch asking for a lube station before it's usable.

The same logic appeals to builders chasing a gaming-adjacent feel without going as far as true low-profile or optical hardware. A shorter runway means less distance between "the switch has registered" and "the stem has stopped moving," which reads as responsive in exactly the way competitive-leaning typists and gamers tend to describe favorably. It's a cheaper, more broadly compatible way to approach that feel than switching housing families entirely.

<Callout type="note">
  Shortened travel is a spec on the switch itself, not a claim about actuation point placement. A 3.3mm switch can still actuate anywhere within that stroke — shortened travel says nothing on its own about where the actuation point sits relative to the stem's starting position, only about how far the stem can go in total.
</Callout>

## Not the same lever as low-profile

It's worth drawing one more line clearly, because the two get grouped together casually: shortened travel is not the same design move as a low-profile keyboard. Low-profile hardware — the kind built around stem families like Kailh Choc — shrinks the entire switch housing height, not just the travel distance, which is what lets the whole keyboard case sit dramatically thinner. A shortened-travel switch like the Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro makes no such claim about its own housing height; it's built to sit in a standard-height case alongside ordinary MX-compatible parts, cutting only the distance the stem covers inside a housing that's otherwise unremarkable. Shorter travel can exist entirely independently of a shorter case — this switch is the proof.

## What to watch

The open question is whether "shortened travel" starts getting marketed as its own spec line the way spring weight already is, rather than something a buyer has to notice buried in a travel-distance field. Right now, a shopper skimming actuation and bottom-out grams past a switch like the Akko V3 Cream Blue Pro can easily miss that the stroke itself is doing a meaningful share of the work in how the switch feels. As more budget-tactile and gaming-leaning switches adopt sub-3.5mm strokes on otherwise standard-height housings, that's the gap worth watching close — a spec sheet that separates "how hard" from "how far" clearly enough that builders stop reaching for the spring when the runway was the thing that actually changed.
