Eight tracker readings in a row, all pointed up, is a real pattern. thock's [Trends Tracker](/trends/tracker) has recorded Alice layout climbing every single week from W35 through W42: -66, -50, -40, -30, -18, -8, -5, 0. That reverses, point for point, the slide this site called in May — [the slow fade it was tracking](/article/alice-layout-decline) at the time. But the May piece didn't just predict the decline. It named two specific products that would need to exist to turn it around. Neither one has shipped. What moved instead was something softer — editorial sentiment and a handful of boutique launches, all still priced at the tier that caused the fade in the first place. That gap between what recovered and what was supposed to cause the recovery is the actual story here.

## Eight weeks, one direction

The [2026-W42 snapshot](/trends/tracker/2026-W42) puts Alice at a score of 0, direction "up," with a spark line that reads as a clean, unbroken climb out of its trough. That trough was real — Alice bottomed out at -66 in W35, deep enough that the May piece built an entire decline thesis around the trajectory. Eight weeks later the layout has clawed back every point of that deficit and landed exactly on the line between negative and positive sentiment.

It's worth being precise about what that means and what it doesn't. A score of 0 is neutral, not growth. Alice hasn't out-performed anything; it has stopped losing ground and stabilized. Compare that to TMR switches, which climbed from 55 to 90 across a similar five-week window this fall — that's a category gaining on an already-positive baseline. Alice's climb is a recovery from a deficit, which is a different and smaller claim than a breakout.

The May piece didn't hedge on what would reverse Alice's decline. It named two things. First, a pre-built Alice at the $200 tier with hot-swap, wireless, and a serious-but-not-precious aesthetic — something that could pull in a typist who isn't already deep in customs. Second, a split-Alice hybrid with adjustable splay angle, which would turn Alice's fixed geometry into a tunable one and let it compete with full-split boards on their own terms rather than asking the user to accept whichever angle the designer picked.

Neither has appeared. No maker thock tracks has shipped a sub-$200 hot-swap, wireless Alice. No split-Alice hybrid with adjustable splay has surfaced on any public roadmap. The two structural fixes the decline thesis called for are, as of the W42 snapshot, still hypothetical.

## What moved instead

<Callout type="note" title="Three events are carrying eight weeks of tracker movement">
  The weekly notes behind this climb cite the same handful of things repeatedly: a Keychron product launch at W36, a run of 2026 buying-guide language from W37 through W41, and two boutique/OEM mentions at W42. That's a thin evidentiary base for a trend this consistent — which is itself worth flagging rather than glossing over.
</Callout>

What actually happened, week by week, is more modest than "the fixes arrived." At W36, Keychron shipped the <Mono>B40</Mono> 75% and the <Mono>B39</Mono> 1800-with-numpad, both scissor-switch boards running ZMK — a concrete, shippable Alice product, but not a hot-swap mechanical board at the $200 tier the May piece described, and not wireless in the sense that mattered to that argument. It was still enough to register as the first up week after a long slide.

From W37 through W41, the tracker notes shift from product launches to editorial framing. 2026 buying guides started describing Alice as a "gateway ergo layout," and by W41 at least one guide called it "the most popular ergonomic stepping stone of the last five years." That's a meaningful shift in how outside coverage frames the layout — from a fading curiosity to a recommended entry point — but it's sentiment, not hardware. Nobody shipped a cheaper board or a tunable-geometry hybrid; the people writing about Alice just started talking about it differently.

By W42, the note attributes the final push to "scattered boutique and OEM activity" — the Epomaker <Mono>Tide Alice</Mono> and the Qwertykeys <Mono>QK Alice Duo</Mono> — while adding, in the same breath, that coverage "stays thin relative to split-ergo and 75%." Even the week that finally crossed the zero line is hedged in its own tracker note. That's not the language of a category that found its breakout product. It's the language of scattered activity nudging a score off its floor.

## Recovery, not reversal

Set Alice's 0 next to its neighbors on the same W42 snapshot and the honest scale of the recovery gets clearer. Split/Ergo layout sits flat at 100 — the ceiling score, the same plateau Hall-effect switches occupy in the switch category. 75% Layout is up at 82. Alice, after undoing an eight-week deficit, is at exactly the midpoint between those two numbers and zero. The [split keyboards](/article/split-ergo-cohort) that the May piece identified as the layout's main competitive pressure haven't cooled off even slightly while Alice was recovering — they've held the top of the board the entire time.

<PullQuote>
  The score stopped falling. The three reasons it was falling are all still true.
</PullQuote>

That's the shape of the honest read. The three structural forces the May piece diagnosed — a fixed geometry that asks the typist to adapt rather than the other way around, premium-only pricing that keeps first-time ergo buyers looking elsewhere, and a split-keyboard ecosystem that's gotten more capable and better-built every year — haven't been addressed by anything that shipped between W35 and W42. What changed is softer: enough incremental activity from Keychron, Epomaker, and Qwertykeys, plus a run of buying guides warming back up to the layout, to stop the bleeding and carry the score back to neutral. A sentiment bounce off a floor and a structural reversal look identical for the first few weeks. They stop looking identical the moment someone asks what would need to be true for the climb to continue past zero.

## What would make this a real reversal

The bar hasn't moved from where the May piece set it. A sub-$200 pre-built Alice with hot-swap and wireless would answer the pricing objection directly. A split-Alice hybrid with adjustable splay would answer the geometry objection by turning Alice from a fixed template into a tunable one. Until one of those two products exists, the most defensible claim is the narrower one: Alice stopped losing, it didn't start winning. The next several tracker snapshots will show whether the climb continues into positive territory on its own, which would suggest the structural story actually is changing, or whether it stalls out near zero once the current run of buying-guide attention and boutique launches runs its course — which would mean the eight-week climb was exactly what it looked like at W35: a layout finding its floor, not its second act.
