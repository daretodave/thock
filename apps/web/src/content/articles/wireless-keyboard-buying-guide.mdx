A keyboard listing that says "wireless" is describing a connector, not a feature. Underneath that one word sit two genuinely different radios — Bluetooth and a proprietary 2.4GHz link through a USB dongle — plus the wired mode most wireless boards still ship with as a third option. They do not perform the same, they do not drain a battery the same way, and they fail in different ones. Picking based on the word "wireless" alone is how a reader ends up with a board that's wrong for their desk.

## Three connections, not one

**Wired** is the baseline everything else gets measured against: a USB cable, a matrix scan, and a report sent to the host every polling interval with nothing in between to add delay. **2.4GHz** uses a small USB receiver — the dongle — and a proprietary radio link that, because it skips the operating system's Bluetooth stack entirely, can behave almost identically to wired. **Bluetooth** talks to the host's built-in radio directly, no dongle required, at the cost of a protocol that was designed for occasional low-power devices before it was ever adapted for something that fires a report on every keystroke.

Most boards worth buying above entry level now ship two or three of these modes in one chassis — a switch or a key combo moves between them — so the real decision isn't which board to buy but which mode to actually leave it in day to day.

## The latency number that actually matters

Wired keyboards typically measure in the range of 1–4ms of total input latency; well-implemented 2.4GHz dongles land close behind, often 1–5ms, because the receiver talks directly to the board's radio without going through the host's Bluetooth stack at all <Source href="https://www.rtings.com/keyboard/tests/latency">RTINGS's keyboard latency testing methodology and results</Source>. Bluetooth is the outlier — the Bluetooth Low Energy connection interval has a spec-defined floor of 7.5ms, and most real keyboards run well above that floor for battery-life reasons, which is why Bluetooth boards commonly measure in the 8–20ms range rather than matching wired.

Polling rate is the other half of the story, and it's the part that's easy to misread from a spec sheet. Wired and 2.4GHz boards can both run at 1,000Hz as a common default, with some gaming-oriented boards pushing higher; Bluetooth is effectively capped by that same connection-interval floor, which is why Bluetooth boards rarely advertise a polling number at all. In practice this means: for a rhythm game, a fighting game, or any competitive title where frame-perfect input timing is part of the skill, 2.4GHz is the wireless mode that actually competes with wired, and Bluetooth is not. For typing, document editing, and ordinary desk work, the entire ladder above sits so far under human reaction time — roughly 150–250ms — that none of it is perceptible. A reader who isn't gaming competitively is not giving anything up by picking Bluetooth on latency grounds alone.

<Callout type="note" title="Polling rate and reporting rate aren't the same claim">
  A board can support a high polling rate in wired mode and a much lower one in 2.4GHz or Bluetooth mode — the radio is a separate bottleneck from the matrix scan. Read the spec sheet's polling number per connection mode, not as a single number for the whole board; a listing that only states one polling rate is usually describing wired mode only.
</Callout>

## Battery life is a duty-cycle question, not a marketing one

The reason Bluetooth boards routinely outlast 2.4GHz boards on a charge has nothing to do with one radio being more "efficient" as a talking point — it's the connection interval again, from the other direction. Bluetooth Low Energy is built to let the radio go quiet between the negotiated connection intervals and wake only to check in or send an event; a 2.4GHz dongle link, by contrast, is built to keep that polling rate high and consistent, which means the radio wakes far more often regardless of whether a key was actually pressed. More wake cycles is more current draw, full stop.

This is why the same tri-mode board can feel like two different products depending on which mode it's left in: 2.4GHz with backlighting on is the mode that needs a charge every few days on a typical board; Bluetooth with backlighting off is the mode that can stretch into weeks. A reader who mostly wants to unplug the cable and forget about the board should default to Bluetooth and reserve 2.4GHz for sessions where the latency actually earns its battery cost.

## Multi-device pairing is Bluetooth's real advantage

This is the tradeoff that gets underweighted next to the latency conversation, and for a large slice of buyers it should be the deciding factor instead. A Bluetooth keyboard can hold multiple paired profiles — commonly three, switched with a key combo — and move between a laptop, a tablet, and a phone in about a second with no cable and no dongle to relocate. A 2.4GHz dongle is a one-to-one pairing: one dongle, one host, and switching to a second computer means physically moving the dongle to that computer's USB port, or buying a second board.

There is a real exception worth knowing before assuming a 2.4GHz keyboard is single-host by nature. Logitech's Bolt and Unifying receivers can pair several peripherals to one receiver <Source href="https://support.logi.com/hc/en-us/articles/1500007361841">Logitech's support documentation on connecting multiple Bolt devices to one receiver</Source> — but that solves a different problem (many mice and keyboards, one receiver, one host), not the host-switching problem a Bluetooth-first buyer usually wants solved. Custom-keyboard 2.4GHz implementations rarely offer even that; most are one board, one dongle, one computer.

A reader who works across a laptop and a desktop, or who wants one board for a phone and a tablet alongside a computer, is buying Bluetooth for this reason before latency ever enters the conversation.

## The dongle is a small, easy-to-lose, hard-to-replace part

The 2.4GHz mode's failure mode is physical rather than electrical. Most consumer and custom-keyboard 2.4GHz receivers are paired to their board at the factory with no re-pairing tool available to the owner — lose the dongle and that mode is gone, not degraded, gone <Source href="https://www.logitech.com/en-us/shop/p/logi-bolt-usb-receiver">Logitech's Bolt receiver product page, describing standardized multi-device pairing as the exception rather than the rule among 2.4GHz receivers</Source>. A board that ships wired and Bluetooth as fallback modes survives a lost dongle without much drama; a board that treats 2.4GHz as its only wireless mode does not.

The dongle is also small enough to travel badly. It rides loose in a laptop bag, gets left in a previous desk's USB port during an office move, or falls out of a pocket — and because it's usually a plain black rectangle with no branding a reader would recognize later, it's easy to throw out by accident once it's separated from the board it belongs to. Boards that include a storage slot on the underside or in the included case remove most of this risk; boards that don't are relying entirely on the owner's own habits.

<Callout type="warn" title="Check for a storage slot before buying dongle-only wireless">
  A board whose only wireless mode is 2.4GHz, with no internal or case slot to store the dongle when not in use, is a worse buy than the spec sheet suggests. That one missing detail is the single biggest predictor of whether a reader is still using the wireless mode a year later.
</Callout>

## How to actually choose

**Competitive gaming or rhythm games**, where frame-perfect timing is part of the skill being tested, want wired first and 2.4GHz second — Bluetooth's connection-interval overhead is the one place on this list where the latency gap is large enough to matter. **General desk work and typing** should default to Bluetooth; the latency difference is imperceptible at typing speed, and the battery and multi-device wins are real. **Anyone splitting time across a laptop, a tablet, and a phone** should treat Bluetooth's profile switching as close to a hard requirement — a 2.4GHz dongle solves none of that problem. **A single dedicated desktop that never travels** is the case where 2.4GHz's latency edge is free to take, as long as the dongle has somewhere to live when the board isn't in use.

None of these are permanent commitments — most tri-mode boards let a reader flip between all three connections with a key combo, which means the right answer to this guide's title question can change by task rather than by purchase. The board that's wrong for a gaming session can still be the right board for the rest of the day, switched over in the time it takes to hold a function key.

## What to watch for next

The gap this guide describes — 2.4GHz's latency edge against Bluetooth's battery and multi-device edge — has been narrowing from the Bluetooth side more than the 2.4GHz side. Later Bluetooth Low Energy revisions push the connection-interval floor down and give firmware more room to negotiate a faster link when the host supports it, which is the direction that eventually closes this gap rather than a radical rework of the 2.4GHz side. Split and wireless firmware specifically is covered in more depth in [the ZMK wireless and split firmware guide](/article/zmk-wireless-split-firmware-guide), including the connection-interval tuning that lets a builder trade some of that battery life back for lower Bluetooth latency on a per-board basis. Until that tradeoff gets easier to make from a configurator instead of a firmware recompile, the practical advice holds: pick the connection for the task that happens most often at that desk, not for the one benchmark number on the box.
