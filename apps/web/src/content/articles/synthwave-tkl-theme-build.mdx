A synthwave keycap set photographs well and says nothing about how a board types. That gap is usually where theme builds go wrong — the caps deliver the look, and whatever's underneath gets forgiven for not living up to it. This build doesn't ask for forgiveness. The board and the switch underneath GMK Laser aren't inspired by the look at all; they're inspired by speed, and the result types faster than it has any business looking like it should.

## The parts

| Role | Part | Why it's here |
|---|---|---|
| Board | <PartReference id="drop-ctrl">Drop CTRL</PartReference> | TKL, aircraft-grade aluminum case, top-mount PCB, hotswap QMK/VIA, per-key RGB |
| Keycaps | <PartReference id="gmk-laser">GMK Laser</PartReference> | Cherry-profile doubleshot ABS, deep-purple base with pink and teal legends, discontinued |
| Switch | <PartReference id="gateron-ink-v2-yellow">Gateron Ink V2 Yellow</PartReference> | Light linear, translucent plum PC housing, POM stem, 35g actuation / 45g bottom-out, factory-lubed |

<Mono>GMK Laser</Mono> is the reason this build exists and the reason it takes longer to assemble than a configurator order. Drop produced the set once, through GMK, starting in November 2018; it hasn't run again since, and its listed status is discontinued. The <Mono>Drop CTRL</Mono> and the <Mono>Gateron Ink V2 Yellow</Mono> both ship in stock. The keycaps are the one part of this build a buyer has to go find.

## A board built to disappear, not to glow

<PartReference id="gateron-ink-v2-yellow">Gateron Ink V2 Yellow</PartReference> is a light, fast, factory-lubed linear — the kind of switch recommended to gaming builds and fast typists specifically because it gets out of the way. There's no bump to read, no click bar to time, just a 35g actuation spring and a POM stem moving through 4.0mm of travel with as little friction as a stock linear offers at its price point. Nothing about that switch is asking to be looked at.

The <Mono>Drop CTRL</Mono> matches that intent at the board level, and it does it with a construction choice that's unusual among the boards this site covers most often. It's top-mounted, not gasket-mounted: the plate bolts straight to the case rather than floating on a compliant gasket boundary. Drop's own description of the board calls out the consequence directly — the top-mount gives "a firmer, more direct typing feel than the gasket boards in the same price range." There's no softening layer between a keystroke and the aluminum underneath it. On a build whose whole visual program is maximalist 1980s neon, the thing a typist's fingers actually feel is minimal: direct, firm, fast, with nothing added for comfort and nothing subtracted for speed.

That's the actual tension in this build, and it's worth naming plainly: a loud, nostalgic, look-at-this keycap set sitting on top of a board and switch that are both about directness and speed, not spectacle. A TKL that looks like it's about to play Tron and types like a word-count sprint.

## The glow comes from the switch, not the cap

The <Mono>CTRL</Mono>'s per-key RGB is the other half of the synthwave fantasy, and it's worth being precise about where that light actually shows up, because the obvious assumption is wrong. <Mono>GMK Laser</Mono> is doubleshot ABS with opaque legends — it's not a shine-through set. The pink and teal lettering on each cap is molded plastic, not a window for backlighting. Per-key RGB under a cap like that doesn't illuminate the legend; it leaks out around the stem well and through whatever light the switch housing itself lets pass.

That's where <Mono>Gateron Ink V2 Yellow</Mono> earns a second look.

<Callout type="note" title="Housing translucency is the actual RGB story here">
  <Mono>Ink V2 Yellow</Mono> runs a semi-translucent deep-plum PC housing, top and bottom. Under per-key lighting, that housing glows from the inside — a diffuse plum-tinted wash around the switch itself, distinct from the sharp, legend-shaped glow a shine-through keycap would produce. With an opaque-legend set like <Mono>GMK Laser</Mono> on top, the switch housing is doing all the glow work the keycap isn't.
</Callout>

It's a genuine point of synergy, but it's a narrower one than it first looks: the RGB reads as ambient glow around each key, not as lit-up lettering. Anyone picturing a shine-through-style build should recalibrate that expectation before ordering anything.

## Build notes: sourcing a set that isn't for sale anymore

Cherry profile is a safe starting point for the clearance question that taller profiles raise on other builds — there's no special-case plate standoff or case-headroom planning required, because Cherry's moderate sculpt was the industry default the <Mono>CTRL</Mono>'s plate and case were designed around in the first place. That part of this build is genuinely low-friction.

Sourcing <Mono>GMK Laser</Mono> itself is not. With the set discontinued since its original run, every unit in circulation is secondhand, and secondhand ABS doubleshot comes with real caveats worth naming honestly rather than glossing over. ABS legends can pick up a shine or a faint grease sheen on heavily used keys faster than PBT does — that's a known tradeoff of the material, not a flaw specific to this set, but it means a six-year-old set has had more time to show it. Listing photos also don't reliably convey legend wear or base-shell condition at the resolution a buyer needs; a set that looks crisp in a seller's photo can read differently once it's under direct light on a desk.

<Callout type="warn" title="Buying GMK Laser means buying on the aftermarket, not a configurator">
  There is no in-stock path to this set. Pricing on the secondhand market reflects its status as a sought-after, discontinued colorway — expect a premium over what it originally sold for, and inspect legend wear and shell condition directly rather than trusting a listing's photos alone.
</Callout>

## Who should skip this build

This isn't a build for a typist who wants a softened, cushioned keystroke — the <Mono>CTRL</Mono>'s top-mount construction is deliberately firmer and more direct than the gasket-mounted boards this column has otherwise favored, and there's no compliance layer to dial that back. It's also not for anyone expecting the RGB to light up the legends themselves; the glow comes from the switch housing, and a buyer picturing a shine-through-style effect will be disappointed by an opaque doubleshot set. And it's not for someone who wants to order a finished parts list in one sitting: <Mono>GMK Laser</Mono> exists only on the secondhand market, at aftermarket prices, on whatever timeline that market sets.

## Verdict

The strength of this build is that nothing underneath the keycaps is trying to compete with them. A gasket-mounted board would blunt the firm, direct feel the <Mono>CTRL</Mono>'s top-mount construction is actually known for; a heavier or tactile switch would slow down a set that's paired here specifically with a switch built for speed; a shine-through keycap set would have made a different, flashier promise about the RGB than this one is set up to keep. What's left is an honest split: a maximalist, nostalgic look riding on top of a minimalist, fast platform, with the board and switch never pretending to be anything louder than they are. For a builder willing to hunt a discontinued set on the aftermarket and type on a firmer board than a gasket-mount crowd is used to, that split is the whole point — not a compromise between two aesthetics, but two different jobs, done well, stacked on top of each other.
