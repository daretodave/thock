## Guides — the trade nobody states up front

**[Low-profile mechanical keyboards: a buying guide](/article/low-profile-keyboards-buying-guide)**

A low-profile board promises a flatter desk and a laptop-like feel, and most buyers get that part of the pitch on sight. What the pitch skips is the catalog you opt out of the moment you commit — most keycap sets and most switches on the market simply don't fit a low-profile stem, and swapping either one later is a much shorter list than the one you're used to.

Read it if you're eyeing a flatter board and haven't checked what you'd actually be able to put on it.

## Ideas — four parts, four switches, one Frankenswitch

**[Frankenswitching: building a custom switch out of other switches' parts](/article/frankenswitching-explained)**

Four parts, four independent axes, and a mix-and-match history older than any factory switch that eventually copied it — the original <Mono>Holy Panda</Mono> was a pile of parts scavenged from two different switches, years before anyone sold it pre-assembled as one SKU. Frankenswitching just names the practice the enthusiast community had already been doing by hand.

Read it if you've got spare housings and spare stems from two different bags and have wondered whether they'd fit.

## Deep dive — three keys, one diode

**[The matrix, the diode, and the three keys that break it](/article/key-matrix-ghosting-deep-dive)**

A keyboard scans its keys in a grid instead of wiring each one to its own pin — an efficient design choice that opens one specific, well-understood failure mode. A diode per switch closes it. A second piece, <Mono>NKRO</Mono> firmware, closes the related failure that diodes alone can't touch.

Read it if you've ever had a key silently fail to register and wanted to know which part of the stack to blame.
