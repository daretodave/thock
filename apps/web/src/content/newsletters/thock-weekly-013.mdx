## Guides — two numbers, not one

**[What a switch's gram number actually tells you before you buy](/article/switch-actuation-weight-buying-guide)**

Every switch spec sheet lists two gram numbers — actuation weight and bottom-out weight — and treating them as one number is the most common mistake in a first switch purchase. The two measure different points in the keystroke, suit typing and gaming differently, and the same gram number reads as a different feel entirely depending on whether the switch is linear, tactile, or clicky.

Read it if you've bought a switch by gram number alone and wondered why it didn't feel like the last one.

## Ideas — a switch built from four other switches' parts

**[Frankenswitching: building a custom switch out of other switches' parts](/article/frankenswitching-explained)**

Frankenswitching builds a custom switch by mixing parts across four independent axes — housing, stem, spring, and leaf/mechanism — pulled from different switch models rather than one factory SKU. The practice predates every factory switch that eventually copied it: the original <Mono>Holy Panda</Mono> was itself a pile of parts from two separate switches, years before anyone sold it as a single product.

Read it if you've bought a Holy Panda and never asked where its parts actually came from.

## Deep dive — same mechanism, four different legs

**[Four Switches, One Mechanism: What Stem Geometry Actually Changes](/article/tactile-stem-geometry-deep-dive)**

<Mono>Durock T1</Mono>, <Mono>Gazzew Boba U4T</Mono>, <Mono>Drop Holy Panda X</Mono>, and <Mono>Gateron Lanes</Mono> all generate their tactile bump the same way — a stem leg crossing a metal contact leaf partway through the keystroke. What separates how they feel isn't the mechanism, it's the geometry of the leg doing the crossing: steep and sharp-edged, rounded D-shaped, a smooth symmetric arc, or stepped and front-loaded.

Read it if you've heard four tactiles described as four different mechanisms that are actually the same one wearing different shapes.
