## Guides — the radio matters more than the word "wireless"

**[Wireless keyboard buying guide: Bluetooth, 2.4GHz, and the latency that actually matters](/article/wireless-keyboard-buying-guide)**

"Wireless" on a spec sheet is really three different radios wearing one label, and each comes with its own latency floor, battery habits, and dongle to misplace. The guide sorts <Mono>Bluetooth</Mono> and <Mono>2.4GHz</Mono> by what they're actually good at rather than by which sounds more premium, and points readers toward matching the radio to the desk instead of the marketing copy.

**[Build of the week: the mission control desk, in Class80, Godspeed, and Box White](/article/mission-control-theme-build)**

## Trends — the sensor caught up, the firmware didn't

**[Hardware caught up to Wooting. Firmware hasn't.](/article/wooting-rapid-trigger-head-start)**

<Mono>Wooting 80HE+</Mono> preorders are the top mover on this week's tracker, climbing from a score of 40 to 80 over eight weeks, but the piece argues the hardware isn't why. Hall-effect sensing is table stakes across the category by September 2026 — half a dozen brands ship it now — so the gap Wooting is actually trading on is software, and rivals still haven't closed it.

Read it if you've assumed rapid trigger is a hardware feature rather than a firmware one.

## News — Keychron's first non-Hall-effect magnetic switch

**[Keychron launches Q16 HE 8K with ceramic-TMR magnetic switches](/article/keychron-q16-he-8k-ceramic-tmr)**

The <Mono>Keychron Q16 HE 8K</Mono> launched on Kickstarter with a ceramic-housed TMR switch and an 8000Hz polling claim bundled into one announcement — the first time the Q-series has bet on tunnel-magnetoresistance sensing instead of the Hall-effect tech Keychron already made the category default. It's a hedge against the same sensor everyone else just adopted.

Read it if you want to know why a brand would move off a standard it helped set.

## Deep dive — the cheapest lever for adding give to a board

**[PCB flex cuts, explained: a flex budget milled straight into the board](/article/pcb-flex-cuts-explained)**

No plate swap, no gasket, no case redesign — a flex cut is just a slot routed through the fiberglass near a switch hole, and it's the cheapest way a designer has to add give to a board that otherwise has none. The piece traces why that cheapness determines exactly where flex cuts show up in a lineup, and what they can't substitute for once a board's stiffness problem is bigger than one slot can fix.

Read it if you've felt a "soft" board and wondered whether the softness came from the plate, the case, or a cut you can't see.
